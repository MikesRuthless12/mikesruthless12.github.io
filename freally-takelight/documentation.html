How it works, step by step.

This manual grows with every version. Right now it covers finding a chain, your presets, Simple mode and its four knobs, A/B, the rack and what each card shows, undo, its four effect returns, Tune, Input Check and its twenty-five modules.

    Getting started

    Takelight runs inside your DAW as a CLAP, VST3 or AU plugin, or on its own as a standalone app. Put it on a vocal track. It opens in Simple mode: search an artist or a genre, pick an era and a role, and the whole chain loads. Until you load a chain or add a module, your audio passes through unchanged.

    There are no downloads before version 1.0.0, and the code is private.

    Finding a chain

    Type in the search at the top — an artist, a nickname or a genre — or press Ctrl+K (Cmd+K on a Mac) from anywhere. The chains appear as you type: each artist with its eras, each era with its years and its roles, such as lead, ad-lib or FX. Choose one and the whole chain loads in order, click-free, while the track plays. The arrow keys move through the list, Enter loads, and Escape closes it.

    With the search empty, it offers the chains you loaded last. An artist with no chain yet offers chains from their genre, and a name it does not know says so and suggests a genre.

    Version 0.5.6 has Drake in three eras — So Far Gone (2009), Take Care (2011) and the modern era (2016–2026) — with an FX chain from the Take Care era, and eighteen genre chains: a lead and an ad-lib for trap (2000s and 2010s), Chicago drill, emo rap, classic R&B, modern pop, rock, country pop and reggaeton. Each chain is named for its artist or genre and its era, and nothing more.

    Your presets

    Save preset… at the top keeps the chain as you have shaped it — every module, knob, send and fader, and Simple's four knobs — under a name you choose. Each preset is a file in Documents › Freally Takelight › Presets, so you can back it up or send it to someone.

    My presets… lists them, each with the chain it was made from, to load, rename, export or delete. Import… brings in a preset someone sent you, numbering its name if you already have one by that name. A file that is not a Takelight preset, is larger than 256 KB or was saved by a newer version is refused, and the list says why.

    Your presets are in the search too, under My presets. When the chain a preset was made from is updated in a later version, the preset loads onto the updated chain and keeps every change you made, and says so.

    Simple mode and the four knobs

    Simple shows the take light, the chain's name, four large knobs and the In and Out meters; Advanced, from the switch at the top, shows the whole rack. Your project remembers which view you left it in, and Settings chooses where a new one opens.

    Tune sets how fast and how naturally the vocal is tuned. Tone makes the chain darker or brighter, around 1 kHz, and the de-esser follows. Space turns every effect send up or down together, by up to 12 dB. Amount sets the whole chain against your dry vocal, from dry at 0 % to the full chain at 100 %.

    With Tune, Tone and Space at the centre and Amount at 100 %, the chain plays as it was made, and every chain loads that way. Each knob is a host parameter, so your DAW can automate it, and a double-click puts it back.

    A/B

    Load reference… opens your system's file dialog, or drop a file on the window. It reads WAV, AIFF, FLAC and MP3, up to 20 minutes, and the bar shows the file's name, its length and its loudness. B plays the reference from where your DAW is (from the start in the standalone app), A is your chain, and each switch is a 20-millisecond crossfade.

    Match loudness plays the reference at your vocal's loudness, so you judge the tone and not the level. The reference is kept in memory only: it is never saved with your project, a preset or anywhere else, and a file on a network drive is refused.

    Compare keeps two versions of your chain. A is the one you are editing; press B and it starts as a copy, so nothing changes; edit it, and A and B each keep their own. Both are saved with your project.

    The rack

    The rack holds the whole chain in signal order, in four rows: the Front end (slots F1 and F2), the Inserts (I1 to I12), the four effect Returns (A to D, slots R1 to R12) and the Bus (B1 to B4).

    Choose a module from an empty slot's menu to add it. Its card shows every control it has: knobs for values, switches, and menus for options. Its header holds its character, when it has more than one, Bypass (B), Solo (S) and the × that takes it out.

    Every change is click-free. A module you add or take out fades in or out, a new character crossfades, and every knob glides, so you can work while the track plays. A bypassed module keeps its latency, so your DAW stays in time.

    Every control works from the keyboard. Tab moves between them; the arrow keys turn a knob, and Shift turns it in bigger steps; Home and End take it to either end; a double-click puts it back to its default.

    A rack holds as many knobs as Takelight can move at once — every slot full of the largest modules would be more — so past that, a module is refused and the rack says it is full.

    What each card shows

    Beside its knobs, each module's card draws what it does, worked out from the same maths that makes the sound. The EQs — Parametric, Console and Program — draw their curve over your vocal as it plays: its spectrum before the EQ, dim, and after it, bright, with the peaks held for a moment. Each band has a dot on the curve: drag it to move the band's frequency and gain together, turn the mouse wheel over it for its width, double-click it to put the band back, or use the arrow keys. A whole drag is one step to undo. The De-esser draws the band it listens to and the cut it makes, and Trim its high-pass.

    The compressors, the Level Rider and the Limiter draw their curve — the level in against the level out — with a dot where your vocal sits on it now, and beneath it the gain reduction of the last four seconds. A steady note puts the dot on the curve; a sharp attack can lift it above for a moment, before the compressor catches it. Drag the threshold along the curve, or use the arrow keys: it turns the knob that sets it. The Gate's card shows the level it hears against its threshold, shaded where it is shut.

    The Delay draws its repeats on a timeline against your project's beat and bar, each as loud as the feedback and filters leave it; Ping-pong's left repeats sit above the line and its right ones below. The Reverb draws its decay — the pre-delay, the early reflections and the tail — and the Reverse Gate its swell. The Doubler, the Chorus and Width show the stereo field from above, and Saturation, the Exciter and Lo-fi show their curve and the harmonics it adds to your vocal as it is now.

    Only the cards on screen draw, and up to eight EQ cards show your vocal's spectrum at once. Tune has its pitch graph instead, and the Shifter its knobs alone.

    Undo and redo

    The arrows at the top undo and redo every change to the sound, up to 100 steps: a knob (a whole drag is one step), a module added or taken out, its character, Bypass and Solo, the sends and faders, Simple's four knobs, the Input knob, a chain or a preset loaded, and Input Check's Apply. Ctrl+Z undoes, and Ctrl+Shift+Z or Ctrl+Y redoes (⌘Z and ⌘⇧Z on a Mac), while Takelight's window has the focus, so your DAW keeps its own shortcuts.

    The view, the theme, the language and the A/B reference are never undone, and neither is your DAW's automation. Each project starts with a fresh history when it opens, and so does each side of the compare when you switch to it.

    Returns and throws

    Each of the four returns, A to D, is fed from the end of the Inserts row by its Send knob, from off to +6 dB. The vocal goes through the return's three slots — a delay and a reverb, say — and the return's fader sets how much of it joins the dry vocal on its way into the Bus.

    Hold a return's Throw key and the vocal is thrown into it: its send rises to 0 dB, and falls back when you let go, over 5 milliseconds either way. Throw A to D are host parameters too, so your DAW can record a throw and play it back — a delay tail on one word.

    A delay set to a note value keeps time with your project's tempo, and follows it when the tempo changes during the song.

    Tune and Auto Key

    Tune pulls each sung note to the nearest note of its key. Retune speed sets how fast: at 0 ms the voice jumps from note to note, the hard, stepped sound; slower, it glides there, and at a few hundred milliseconds the tuning is hard to hear. Humanize lets long notes settle more slowly, and Flex keeps the scoops and vibrato that wander furthest from the note. The voice's formants stay where they are, so it keeps its character, and breaths and consonants pass through untouched.

    The Key menu starts on Auto Key: Tune listens to your vocal and settles on its key within about four seconds of singing, tuning to the nearest semitone until then, while the take light glows amber. The card says the key it hears, and Lock keeps it. Or choose Chromatic, or any of the 24 keys yourself. If no key is heard after 20 seconds of singing, the card asks you to choose one.

    The pitch graph shows the last four seconds: the pitch you sang as a thin line and the pitch Tune sends on as a green one, over a line for the note it is pulling to.

    To hear a note before it tunes it, Tune waits 21 milliseconds, and Takelight reports that to your DAW, so the vocal stays in time with the beat. While you record, that wait would be in your headphones, so the Tracking host parameter, on Auto, drops Tune to 4 milliseconds whenever your DAW records, and the card says so. Set Tracking to On or Off to choose yourself.

    A rack holds one Tune, because one Tune tunes the whole vocal.

    Auto Key can find the key from the beat instead. Send the beat to Takelight's sidechain input in your DAW and set Listens to, on Tune's card, to Sidechain: Tune follows the beat's chords. Your project remembers the choice, and Settings sets it for new ones. While nothing plays on the sidechain, Tune listens to your vocal, and the card says so.

    Input Check

    Input Check, at the top of the rack, sets you up before you record. Press it and sing or play the take for ten seconds; the take light glows amber while it listens. It needs at least two seconds of voice, and asks again when it heard too little.

    Then it shows what it heard — the peak, the level, the loudness, the noise floor, the rumble under 80 Hz, the sibilance and the plosives — and what it suggests: the Input knob, to bring your voice to a healthy level, and the gate's threshold, the high-pass and the de-esser's threshold. It sets only the modules your chain already has; one it lacks says Not in this chain, and nothing is added for you.

    Nothing changes until you press Apply, and Undo takes the whole Apply back in one step. Discard leaves everything as it was. If the take clips, it asks you to lower your interface's gain, because clipping can't be undone after recording.

    Automation

    Each slot owns 24 host parameters, named after the slot (F1 to B4), and a module's knobs and switches take them in order, so your DAW can automate them. The list of parameters never changes when you add, swap or remove a module.

    A few settings are not automated, because changing them mid-note would click: an EQ band's type and slope, a filter's slope, and modes such as the de-esser's detection. They are saved with your project all the same.

    The take light and the meters

    The take light at the top of the rack glows green while a module is in the rack and your voice is coming in, flashes red when the input clips, and stays dark otherwise. It turns amber while Tune's Auto Key is listening for the key, and while Input Check listens.

    The In and Out meters on either side show the left and right channels, and beside the In meters the Input knob sets the level coming in, from −24 to +24 dB; your DAW can automate it. Each has a clip light that stays lit until you click it. A compressor's card shows how much it is turning the vocal down, while it is.

    The modules

    Version 0.5.6 has twenty-five modules. Each is Takelight's own design, and none claims to be any other product.

    Tune

    Pitch correction to a key — Auto Key, Chromatic or one of the 24 — with Retune speed from 0 to 400 ms, Humanize and Flex, and a graph of the pitch sung and tuned. One per rack; see Tune and Auto Key above.

    Trim

    Gain, polarity and a high-pass filter with a choice of slope: the first thing in most chains.

    Parametric EQ

    Eight bands, each a bell, a shelf, a cut or a notch, with cuts from 6 to 96 dB per octave. Its knobs show one band at a time — choose the band by its number — and every band has its dot on the curve.

    Console EQ

    A mixing console's channel EQ: high- and low-pass filters and four bands, in four characters — British E, British G, Class A and Proportional Q.

    Program EQ

    Broad, gentle boosts and cuts at the low and high ends, and a tube stage for warmth.

    Opto Compressor

    Smooth levelling that follows the performance, from one Peak reduction knob, in an Opto and a Tube opto character, compressing or limiting.

    FET Compressor

    Fast and forward, with fixed ratios and an All setting for aggressive compression, and a Mix control for parallel compression.

    VCA Compressor

    Clean and precise, in a Bus and a Channel character, with RMS or peak detection, a sidechain high-pass and a Mix control.

    Vari-mu Compressor

    A tube compressor whose ratio rises the harder it works, so it starts gently and holds firm, with six time constants; 5 and 6 let go more slowly the longer it has been compressing.

    Punch Compressor

    A character compressor in five characters, from Normal to Distortion 3, with Nuke for extreme compression and a Mix control.

    Vox Leveler

    Evens out a vocal's level with just a threshold and an output.

    Level Rider

    Rides the vocal's level toward a target loudness — up for the quiet words, down for the loud ones — within a range and at a speed you set, and leaves the breaths between phrases alone. An optional 10 ms look-ahead lets it move just before a word; it adds 10 ms of latency, which Takelight reports to your DAW.

    De-esser

    Tames harsh "s" sounds, split-band or wideband, with a bell or a shelf, and relative or absolute detection.

    Gate

    A gate or an expander between phrases, with hold, hysteresis and sidechain filters so breaths and bleed do not open it.

    Saturation

    Tape, Tube, Transformer and Inflate, oversampled so the harmonics it adds stay clean.

    Exciter

    Adds harmonics above a frequency you choose — odd, even or a blend of both — for presence and air. Only the harmonics are added; the vocal itself passes through untouched.

    Lo-fi

    Fewer bits and a lower sample rate, from 24 bits down to 4 and from 48 kHz down to 1 kHz, with dither, an anti-alias filter, drive and a Mix control.

    Limiter

    A true-peak limiter: the output never rises above its ceiling, even between samples.

    Delay

    Five characters — Digital, Tape, Analog, Ping-pong and Slap. Its time is set in milliseconds or as a note value from 1/64 to two bars, triplets and dotted notes among them, in time with your project. Filters in the feedback, ducking under the vocal, and width; above 100 % feedback the repeats swell and hold instead of running away.

    Reverb

    Five characters — Plate, Room, Hall, Chamber and Ambience — with pre-delay, a decay from 0.1 to 20 seconds, size, damping, diffusion, modulation and filters on the return. The decay is the time the tail takes to fall by 60 dB.

    Reverse Gate

    The reverse reverb: after each sound, a wash that swells over the gate length, from 50 ms to a second, and stops dead. It never looks ahead, so it adds no latency and works while you record.

    Doubler

    Turns one take into two, three or four voices, each a little late, drifting a little in time and pitch, and spread across the stereo field, in a Tight and a Wide character. Summed to mono, it stays close to the vocal's own level.

    Chorus

    A two-voice chorus, one voice rising in pitch as the other falls, with rate, depth, mix and drive; at a 50 % mix it keeps the level and sums to mono cleanly.

    Width

    Narrows or widens the stereo image, from mono at 0 % to 200 %, without changing the mono sum, and a Haas delay of up to 30 ms on the right side to spread a mono vocal.

    Shifter

    Moves the voice up or down by up to two octaves, its formants moved on their own by up to an octave, with a Mix control: the ad-lib an octave down. Like Tune, it waits 21 milliseconds, or 4 while Tracking.

    Saved with your project

    Your project keeps the chain, every module and knob, the view, Simple's four knobs, both sides of the compare and what Tune listens to. If a project's settings cannot be read, it opens on the default chain and says so once, above the stage, until you dismiss it.

    The licence agreement

    The first time Takelight opens it shows its licence agreement and its disclaimer. Until you agree, it does nothing but pass your audio through unchanged, so declining never silences a track.

    Your answer is kept on your computer and shared by every open copy of Takelight, in every DAW and in the standalone app. You can read the agreement again and accept it at any time.

    Settings

    Settings chooses light or dark and the language, among other things. Your choices belong to the computer, not the project: they apply to every copy of Takelight on it.

    Languages

    Takelight and this site speak eighteen languages, with Arabic laid out right to left. Artist names are never translated.

    This is a summary. Takelight shows the full disclaimer with its licence agreement.

    Privacy and the network

    The plugin and the standalone app never go online: no account, no telemetry, and no networking code, which a build gate refuses. Your vocals never leave your computer, and a reference file you load for A/B is kept in memory only.

    From version 1.0, a separate helper checks for updates and activates your licence — only when you ask. It never downloads or installs anything.

    When something goes wrong

    Write to freallyproducts@gmail.com. When Takelight itself fails, the message it shows offers Report: it opens a page in your own browser showing everything it would send, and nothing leaves your computer until you press Send. After a crash, the next launch offers the same.
